    01

    What I understand

    In one sentence: K‑Meets is a booking app that pairs you with one stranger (or a small, gender‑balanced group) at a vetted café at a time you both have free, keeps you anonymous until you physically arrive, and turns that first meeting into an ongoing community of people who keep meeting up.

    The problem it solves

    Urban Indians are surrounded by people and starving for real conversation. Dating apps optimise for romance and photos; Meetup.com‑style events are generic and unsafe-feeling; WhatsApp groups have no structure. Nobody has built a curated, safety‑first way for two strangers who share interests to sit down and talk — friendship, mentorship, or just company, not necessarily romance.

    Why K‑Meets specifically

    You've run two Hyderabad cafés (Tree Stories Café and Café Kanmani) for five years and already ran a manual version of this: a 12‑hour, ticketed event with 40 paying attendees and 100% retention, before any software existed. That's real evidence people will pay for curated connection, and it gives K‑Meets two things most pre‑seed apps don't have: 20+ real venue‑owner relationships to onboard partners fast, and first‑hand knowledge of what a café needs (footfall, table turn, no‑show handling) to say yes.

    The core mechanic

    A user marks when they're free and which venue tier they'd like. The system matches two (or a small group of) users with overlapping availability and shared interests. Until both people physically check in at the venue, neither can see the other's name or photo — only age, gender, relationship status and top interests. The venue host confirms arrival (an ID glance, not a stored ID), which unlocks identities and starts the meetup. Afterwards, users rate the experience, can choose to stay connected, and are funnelled toward paid community events (day trips, trail runs) that become the retention engine.

    How the business makes money

    Three streams: an entry ticket per booking (₹249–₹2,049 depending on venue tier, blended average ₹450), a 5% commission on the food bill at the venue (venues keep 95%, which is the pitch to get them to say yes), and ticketed community events (already validated at ₹1,100/head). Gross margin per meetup works out to roughly 97%, because the venue — not K‑Meets — carries the physical cost of space, food and staff.

    05

    Technical architecture

    Three applications sharing one backend, in plain terms: frontend is what people click on, backend is the program running on a server that holds the real logic and talks to the database, and the database is where all the data actually lives permanently.

    Consumer app

    What members use to sign up, book, get matched, and check in. Runs on a phone.

    Partner dashboard

    What venue hosts use to see bookings and check members in. Runs in a browser, works fine on a phone screen too.

    Admin dashboard

    What you and Trust & Safety use to run the business. Runs in a browser.

    Backend API

    One shared program all three apps talk to. Owns every rule about who can see what.

    Database

    PostgreSQL — the single source of truth for users, bookings, matches, payments.

    Background workers

    Handles matching, reminders, and payouts without making anyone wait on a spinning screen.

    How a booking actually flows through the system

    The consumer app never talks to the database directly — it only ever talks to the backend API, and the backend is the only thing with a key to the database. When a member books a slot, the app sends that request to the API; the API checks the member is KYC-verified and has paid, writes a booking row to Postgres, and puts a "try to match this booking" job on a queue (Redis). A background worker picks that job up a few seconds later, looks for another booking with overlapping availability and similar interests, and if it finds one, marks both as matched — all without the member sitting on a loading screen waiting for a live search to finish. The two matched users' apps get a push notification. Nothing about either user's name or photo is ever sent to the other's device until the venue host taps "checked in" for both of them at the venue — that check runs entirely on the backend, so there's no way to bypass it by inspecting or replaying an app request.

    Rules baked into the design

    No raw government ID numbers are ever stored — kyc_verifications keeps a provider reference and a status, nothing else, per your Master Instruction's data-minimization requirement and India's DPDPA law (§08). Every table that can be deleted by a user (like an account) is soft-deleted first — marked hidden, not physically erased — so financial and safety records stay intact for the legally required retention period, then hard-deleted on a schedule. All money and identity-sensitive tables get created-at/updated-at timestamps and go through versioned migrations (tracked, reversible database changes) rather than being edited by hand, so nothing changes in production without a record of exactly what and when.

    08

    Security plan

    This is the section your Investor Document calls "the #1 investor concern," and it's also the one with real-world stakes: this app introduces two strangers in person. Software can reduce risk; it can't eliminate it. Both halves matter below.

    Stop & flag — this needs a lawyer, not just an engineer

    K-Meets facilitates real-world, in-person meetings between strangers, and collects biometric-adjacent KYC data (a face video plus a government ID). That combination touches consumer-safety liability, India's DPDPA data-protection law, and RBI-adjacent KYC-vendor rules. I can build every technical safeguard listed below, and I can flag the compliance shape of the problem — but I'm not able to tell you your app is "legally compliant" or "not liable if something goes wrong," and neither should any AI. Before you take your first paying user, get an Indian counsel to review: your Terms of Service and liability language, your DPDPA consent flows, and whether event/meetup liability insurance makes sense. Please don't let marketing copy (including "5-layer safety architecture") imply a guarantee of safety that the product can't actually make.

    What's enforced in the software

    AreaWhat we build
    LoginPhone OTP only (no passwords to leak); OTP requests rate-limited (max 5/hour per number) to stop abuse; short-lived session tokens that can be revoked on logout or a new device login
    Identity revealName/photo fields are excluded from the API response itself until both check-ins are recorded server-side — not just hidden in the app's UI, which a user could bypass by reading the raw network traffic
    AuthorizationEvery request that touches a booking, match, report or SOS alert checks server-side that the requester is actually a participant — prevents one member ever pulling up another's data by guessing an ID (this class of bug is called IDOR, and it's one of the most common ways apps like this leak data)
    KYC dataNo ID numbers stored; raw KYC video held only as long as the provider needs to verify, then deleted on schedule; only a verified/rejected flag persists
    PaymentsRazorpay's hosted checkout — card details never reach our servers; every webhook is signature-verified before we trust it; duplicate payment confirmations are rejected via idempotency keys
    UploadsVenue/profile photos validated by actual file content (not just the filename extension), size-capped, stored privately with signed, expiring URLs
    Admin accessRole-based: Trust & Safety can moderate but not touch finance; every admin action is written to the audit log, including who viewed what
    Input handlingEvery field validated on the server (never trusting what the app sends), parameterised database queries throughout to rule out SQL injection, output-escaped to rule out script injection
    SecretsAPI keys and database credentials live in environment variables, never in code; a .env.example file with placeholder names ships in the codebase, real values never do

    What's enforced by process (needs you, not code)

    The CCTV your deck relies on is the venue's own, not something K-Meets installs or controls — we can verify it exists during onboarding, but we can't guarantee it's working or monitored on any given day. The three-strike/ban system only works if reports get reviewed quickly, which needs a real person (your Trust & Safety hire) checking the queue, especially in the first months when it's likely just you. And Video KYC verifies that someone holds a real PAN or Passport — it's a strong deterrent against catfishing, but it isn't a criminal background check.

    Performance plan

    The goal at your Year-1 scale (1,200 monthly active users) is not "handle internet-scale traffic" — it's "never make someone wait on a spinner for something that should be instant."

    LayerWhat we do
    MatchingRuns as a background job on a Redis-backed queue, never inside the request a user is waiting on — booking confirms instantly, the match notification arrives moments later
    Venue browsingVenue lists and photos are cached (Redis, short TTL) since they change rarely compared to how often they're viewed
    Database queriesIndexes on every column we filter by often (venue + date on bookings, status on reports, user on payments); pagination on every list endpoint so we never return an unbounded result set
    ImagesResized on upload and served through CloudFront (a CDN) so a venue photo loads from a nearby server, not all the way from AWS's origin every time
    Third-party callsKYC and payment provider calls run with explicit timeouts and are never retried infinitely on failure — a stuck provider call fails visibly instead of hanging a request

    Deployment plan

    In plain terms: deployment is the process of taking finished code and putting it on a real server the public can reach. Here's how it'll work, end to end.

    Three environments. Local is the copy running on the development machine while building — nobody else can see it. Staging is a private copy on AWS that looks exactly like production but uses test/sandbox Razorpay and KYC keys, so we can try a real booking end-to-end without spending real money or creating a real KYC record. Production is the live app real users touch, using real payment and KYC keys.

    Domain & HTTPS. You'll need to buy a domain (for example, kmeets.in or kmeets.app) — I'll walk you through exactly where to click when we get there. Once pointed at AWS, HTTPS (the padlock in the browser, meaning traffic is encrypted) is issued and renewed automatically — no manual certificate management.

    How an update goes live. Every code change runs through an automated pipeline (GitHub Actions) that lints it, type-checks it, runs the test suite, and only then builds and deploys it — first to staging automatically, then to production after an explicit approval step. A change that fails any of those checks simply cannot reach production.

    Rolling back. Every deployment is tagged, so reverting to the previous working version is a single command, typically live again in under two minutes. Database changes are written to be backward-compatible where possible (add new columns/tables rather than destructively changing existing ones in the same step) specifically so a rollback doesn't get stranded by a database that's moved ahead of the code.

    Backups. The production database takes automated daily snapshots with 30-day retention and point-in-time recovery (can restore to almost any minute in the last few days, not just the last daily snapshot). A backup we've never tried to restore isn't a real backup, so we'll run an actual restore drill before go-live and again roughly every quarter.

    Open questions

    Per your Master Instruction, I'm only asking what genuinely needs your judgment — not technical trivia I can reasonably decide myself. I've sent these as a quick-choice form alongside this document; here's the reasoning behind each.

    1. Consumer app: native mobile app, or mobile-web first?

    Your deck says React Native, iOS + Android. That gets you into app stores, which reads well to investors and users alike, but adds real time (app store review, 1–2 weeks per release, occasional rejections to fix) and cost (developer accounts) before your first user can install anything. This is genuinely your call because it trades launch speed against how the product presents to investors and users.

    2. How should I pace this build?

    Your Master Instruction is explicit that I shouldn't build the whole thing blindly in one giant pass. I'd like to build phase by phase (project setup → database → auth → core booking/matching → community layer → admin/finance → polish → security/performance audit → deployment prep), showing you a working, testable result after each phase before moving to the next. The alternative is building as much as possible autonomously between check-ins. The phased approach is slower to reach "everything," but catches misunderstandings early and matches your own instructions — I'd recommend it, but it's your project.

    3. Where are you starting from operationally?

    This tells me whether to wire the code against real sandbox accounts now, or build every external integration (payments, KYC, SMS, cloud hosting) behind a swappable interface using safe test doubles until real accounts exist. Specifically: do you have a registered company yet (Razorpay and most India KYC vendors require one to issue even sandbox/test credentials in some cases), and have you already signed up for any of Razorpay, Signzy/Digio, MSG91, or AWS?
